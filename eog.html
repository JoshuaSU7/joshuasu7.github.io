EOG Eye Tracking & Blink Detection

Electrooculography-based eye tracking and blink detection for mouse control — giving people who can only move their eyes a way back to a computer.

Python Arduino Signal Processing Real-time Analysis
View on GitHub

Overview

What if you could control a mouse using only your eyes? For most people that is a gimmick. For someone with locked-in syndrome — a form of paralysis where eye movement is the only motion left — it is the difference between having a way to communicate and not having one.

Electrooculography (EOG) picks up the electrical potential generated by the eye as it moves or blinks. Because that signal carries both gaze direction and blink events, it can drive cursor movement and clicks at the same time.

Approach

My team and I started with the vertical axis only. We first found the optimal electrode placement — directly above and below the eye — then acquired and filtered the signal using an Arduino, a biosignal amplifier, and a bandpass filter from 0.1 Hz to 30 Hz.

Investigating the relationship between EOG signal magnitude and gaze angle turned up something useful: it is linear. The further the eyes move, the higher the peak in the signal. That meant a simple linear regression on incoming signal peaks was enough to drive cursor movement. We also found blink peaks are significantly larger than eye-movement peaks, so straightforward thresholding separates the two — and blinks become clicks.

Calibration

Physiology varies between people, so every user has a slightly different magnitude-to-angle slope and a different blink threshold. We built calibration programs for both.

For eye movement, the user plays a short tracking game: press a key, a point moves, follow it with your eyes. The software records EOG in the background and fits the linear relationship between signal magnitude and point distance once the game ends. For blinks, we simply ask the user to blink several times and take the average peak value.

Results & Progress

Blink detection is reliable. Eye tracking is the harder half, and the detection algorithm has been through several revisions. The current version works like this:

  1. When the signal is flat — no eye movement — establish a baseline average, plus symmetric upper and lower bounds around it.
  2. When the signal rises and breaks through the upper bound, watch for the inflection point where it turns from rising to falling. That point is the true peak.
  3. Subtract the baseline from that peak and feed the difference through the linear regression to get the corresponding mouse movement.

Next steps are tuning parameters in the real-time algorithm and extending it to the horizontal axis. In parallel, the mechanical subteam is designing a wearable glasses form factor for the electrodes and the electrical subteam is building a custom PCB with better amplification and filtering.
