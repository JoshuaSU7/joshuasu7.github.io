Six-Channel Sensor Interface PCB

A 24 V avionics test board that powers and reads six 4–20 mA current-loop sensors, digitizes them with an external ADC, and hands the data to an STM32 over SPI.

Altium Designer PCB Design Power Electronics Mixed-Signal GT Yellow Jacket Space Program

Overview

Rockets are full of sensors: tank pressure, chamber pressure, line pressure, temperature and thrust. Most of the industrial transducers used for these report their reading as a 4–20 mA current rather than a voltage, because a current survives long, noisy cable runs far better. This board is a smaller version of the sensor electronics on the Yellow Jacket Space Program rocket. It takes a 24 V supply, powers six current-loop sensors, measures each one and turns the result into data a microcontroller can use.

I designed it end to end in Altium Designer, from schematic capture and component selection through current budgeting, trace sizing, floorplanning and routing.

System Architecture

The easiest way to read the board is to follow two paths. Power enters as 24 V, is protected, then stepped down to 5 V and 3.3 V for the electronics. Data starts at each sensor, becomes a voltage in its channel circuit, is digitized by the ADC, and is read by the STM32.

The schematic is hierarchical. Each major function lives on its own sheet, and all six sensor channels are instances of a single reviewed channel sheet. A fix to one channel applies to all six automatically, and the top level stays readable.

Input Protection

Power arrives on a keyed Molex 43045-0406 connector, with two contacts each for 24 V and ground. Before it reaches anything else, it passes through three protection parts, and each one guards against a different kind of fault:

  • Fuse (F1), in series: opens the supply path if a short, failed part or solder bridge draws sustained excess current, before traces, connectors or wiring overheat.
  • TVS diode (D1, SMAJ26A), to ground: stays off at normal voltage, then clamps short overvoltage spikes such as ESD from plugging in a cable. It sits on the fused side, so a TVS that conducts for too long will blow the fuse instead of burning up.
  • Bulk capacitor (C1, 10 µF / 50 V): local energy storage that steadies the input against harness resistance and inductance when the load changes quickly.

The protected output is the P24V_SAFE rail, which feeds everything downstream.

Power Conversion

Power is converted in two stages. Going straight from 24 V to 3.3 V with a linear regulator would burn most of the input power as heat, so a switching buck converter does the large step first, and a small LDO handles the last one.

Buck converter: 24 V → 5 V

A Maxim MAX17503 synchronous buck converter switches the 24 V input and filters it through a 22 µH inductor into a regulated 5 V rail. The supporting parts follow the datasheet’s reference design:

  • Input capacitors (3 × 1 µF, 100 V) close to VIN supply the converter’s fast switching current.
  • Output capacitors (2 × 22 µF plus 1 µF) smooth the inductor current into a steady DC output.
  • Feedback divider (143 kΩ / 31.6 kΩ, 0.1%) sets the output to 5 V against the internal 0.9 V reference.
  • Bootstrap capacitor (100 nF, BST to LX) drives the high-side switch, and a soft-start capacitor (22 nF) ramps the output gently at power-up to limit inrush.

LDO: 5 V → 3.3 V

An ST LDL1117S33R fixed-output LDO makes the 3.3 V rail for the STM32 and the ADC’s digital side. The step is only 1.7 V, so a linear regulator is simple and quiet, and it needs just an input and an output capacitor (1 µF and 4.7 µF) for stability.

Microcontroller

The STM32H573RIT6 is the board’s brain. In a finished system its firmware would configure the ADC, read each channel, convert currents to engineering units, flag out-of-range readings and forward the data. The hardware around it covers what it needs to run and be programmed:

  • Decoupling: a 4.7 µF bulk capacitor plus a 100 nF capacitor per VDD pin, with separate filtering on the analog supply (VDDA).
  • VCAP: 2.2 µF capacitors on the internal core regulator’s output pins, as ST specifies.
  • Boot configuration: BOOT0 is pulled low through 10 kΩ, so the chip always starts from its own flash.
  • JTAG / SWD connector: a 14-pin debug header with pull-ups on the debug lines, for programming and on-chip debugging.
  • SPI series resistors: 47 Ω resistors on each SPI line slow the signal edges, reducing ringing and overshoot on the bus.

ADC

The sensors produce analog voltages, so a Texas Instruments ADS114S06 (16-bit, six analog inputs) digitizes them. Each sensor channel gets its own input, AIN0–AIN5, measured against AINCOM. The ADC uses its internal 2.5 V reference, which comfortably covers the 0.4–2.2 V range the sensor channels produce. Its supplies are split: the analog side runs from 5 V and the digital and I/O side from 3.3 V, each with its own decoupling.

The STM32 talks to the ADC over SPI (SCLK, MOSI, MISO and a chip select). The ADC also has a nDRDY line that tells the STM32 when a new conversion is ready, so the microcontroller doesn’t have to keep polling.

4–20 mA Sensor Channels

A 4–20 mA sensor sets its current to represent the measurement. For a 0–500 psi transducer, 4 mA means 0 psi and 20 mA means 500 psi. Starting at 4 mA instead of zero gives a live zero: a broken or unplugged sensor reads near 0 mA, which is clearly different from a valid minimum reading.

Each of the six identical channels does three jobs:

  • Current-limited sensor power: an LT3092 current source feeds the sensor from 24 V. Its 22 kΩ and 10 Ω set resistors cap the loop at about 22 mA, just above the normal range. In normal operation the sensor sets the current. If the sensor or its cable shorts, the LT3092 holds the current at about 22 mA instead of letting the 24 V supply dump into the fault.
  • Current to voltage: the return current flows through a 100 Ω shunt to ground, so 4–20 mA becomes 0.4–2.0 V. Even at the 22 mA limit the shunt dissipates only about 49 mW.
  • Filtering: a 1 kΩ / 100 nF RC low-pass filter (cutoff about 1.6 kHz) removes high-frequency noise picked up by the sensor wiring before the signal reaches the ADC.

Current Accounting and Trace Sizing

Before routing, I worked out the worst-case current on every power rail, added a 1.5× safety margin, and sized each trace for an acceptable voltage drop (3% of the rail) and temperature rise. Current is traced back from the loads:

  • The 3.3 V rail is dominated by the STM32 (~350 mA). The ADC’s draw is negligible.
  • An LDO’s input current equals its output current, so the 5 V rail carries the same 350 mA.
  • The 24 V side is sized by power, not current: 350 mA × 5 V ≈ 1.75 W out of the buck means only about 73 mA in at 24 V. The six sensor loops add up to 6 × 22 mA = 132 mA, for about 205 mA in total.
  • The LDO dissipates (5 − 3.3) V × 350 mA ≈ 0.6 W. At 120 °C/W that is a 72 °C rise in the part itself, so its traces were given a tighter 5 °C budget to avoid adding more heat.

PCB Layout

Layout started with floorplanning instead of routing. Each schematic sheet came into the PCB as an Altium room, which made it easy to keep each functional block together: input and protection at the connector edge, power conversion along the top, the STM32 in the middle, and the ADC with the six sensor channels in the lower half, near their connectors. Once the floorplan made the ratsnest short and untangled, the individual parts were placed and routed.

  • Input protection at the edge: the fuse, TVS and bulk capacitor sit right next to J1, so very little of the board is unprotected and the TVS has a short, low-inductance path to ground.
  • Tight buck layout: the input capacitors, inductor and switch node are packed close to the MAX17503 to keep the high-current switching loop small.
  • Analog kept away from noise: the short analog sensor traces are kept away from the buck’s switching node and other high-current paths.
  • Decoupling at the pins: each decoupling capacitor sits right at the supply pin it serves, keeping its loop area small.
  • Sized power traces: every power rail is routed at or above the minimum width from the table above.
  • Ground pours and stitching: both outer layers carry ground pours, stitched to the internal ground planes with vias for a continuous, low-impedance return path.
